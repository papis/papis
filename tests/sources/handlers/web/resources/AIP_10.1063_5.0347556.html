      An implication of Faraday's Law is that the rate of change of the poloidal flux relative to the toroidal flux associated with a magnetic surface is given by the loop voltage. The emphasis in the tokamak literature on the poloidal flux, almost to the exclusion of the toroidal magnetic flux, has led to fundamental mistakes in understanding magnetic field evolution, especially as it affects plasma disruptivity. Designs of tokamak power plants include a divertor that is defined by a separatrix at the plasma edge. The use of the toroidal flux clarifies the descriptions of such plasmas. This paper explores the effects of a separatrix on plasma parameters that are used for assessing disruptivity: the edge safety factor and the internal inductance. The exploration of these effects includes an analytic model, which illustrates why the use of the poloidal instead of the toroidal flux introduces a significant definitional uncertainty in what is meant by the edge safety factor and the internal inductance.

      I. INTRODUCTION

      The natural radial coordinate in tokamaks and stellarators is a magnetic flux that is associated with each toroidal surface. For stellarators, the standard choice is the toroidal magnetic flux ψt enclosed within each surface. For tokamaks, the standard choice is ψpin⁠, normally denoted by ψ⁠, which is the poloidal flux that is internal to the plasma and produced by the plasma current. The flux ψpin has a subtle relationship to the poloidal flux ψp associated with a toroidal surface, which is the magnetic flux that passes through the central hole of that toroidal surface, Fig. 1.

      Equation (1) for the relation between ψpin and ψp may seem unnecessarily complicated. However, as explained in the paper Constraints on the magnetic field evolution in tokamak power plants,1 the failure to recognize the importance of Eq. (1) led to interpreting simple but misleading models of plasma evolution as more rigorous than exact results from Faraday's Law. This paper is essentially an appendix to Ref. 1, which discussed the effect of a separatrix using results derived here.

      Figure 1 illustrates the definition of the toroidal magnetic flux ψt≡∫B→·da→φ and the poloidal flux ψp≡−∫B→·da→θ⁠. Reference 2 proves, using only Faraday's Law and mathematics, that during any interval of time in which a particular magnetic surface persists that encloses the toroidal magnetic flux ψt⁠, the poloidal flux ψp evolves as
      ∂ψp(ψt,t)∂t=Vℓ(ψt,t),
      (2)
      where
      Vℓ(ψt,t)≡limL→∞ ∫−LLE→·dℓ→∫−LL∇→(φ2π)·dℓ→,
      (3)
      and dℓ→ is the differential distance along a magnetic field line. The loop voltage Vℓ gives the slippage of the poloidal relative to the toroidal magnetic flux.
      Equation (2) for the slippage of the poloidal flux relative to the toroidal requires the retention of the toroidal flux ψt in an analysis of the evolution of the poloidal field. Although the proof of Eq. (2) is non-trivial, the importance of the definition ψpin≡Ψpax(t)−ψp is trivially proven at ψpin=0⁠, which is the magnetic axis of a tokamak. Faraday's Law plus Stokes' Theorem imply that the magnetic flux enclosed by the magnetic axis is
      dΨpaxdt=∮axE→·dℓ→,
      (4)
      with dℓ→ the differential distance along the circular magnetic axis.

      Ψpax(t)=Ψpsol(t)+Ψppl(t),
      (5)

      Ψppl(t)=−(Ψpin(t)+Ψpex(t)),

      It has long been known3 that disruptions occur when the profile of the plasma current profile is too peaked, which gives tearing modes, or insufficiently peaked, which gives external kinks. A measure of the peakedness of the current profile is the internal inductance ℓi for poloidal field energy, Eq. (11). The feasibility of tokamak power plants depends on adequate disruption avoidance4 and requires the internal poloidal flux Ψpin lie in the range ΔΨpin that is consistent with acceptable current profiles.

      Unfortunately, ΔΨpin/Ψppl≪1⁠, so careful control, which is easier in tokamak experiments than in power plants, seems required. In particular, the loop voltage Vℓ must be essentially constant across the plasma.1 In the absence of strong current drive or bootstrap current, the current profile is determined by the profile of the plasma resistivity, η⁠. The primary dependencies of η are the electron temperature and Zeff⁠, which is determined by the plasma impurities and their state of ionization.

      The power required to maintain the plasma in an economic power plant should be ≲5% of the power passing through the plasma, but in experiments, it is the total power except for the internal resistive heating. The limitation on the power required for maintenance of a fusion power plant implies that both the level external control of the temperature profile by heating and the level of current profile control by external current drive are small.1 

      The rate of change of the flux in the central solenoid, dΨpsol/dt⁠, is directly controllable, but the rate of change of the poloidal flux enclosed by the magnetic axis, dΨpax/dt⁠, is not—certainly not to the accuracy required to avoid disruptions.

      The toroidal flux ψt has had little prominence in the literature on tokamaks despite Eq. (2) requiring its use to precisely determine the evolution of the poloidal magnetic field in toroidal plasmas.

      The use of the toroidal flux ψt enclosed by a magnetic surface also leads to simpler and more precise definitions of the internal inductance, ℓi⁠, and of the edge safety factor when the magnetic surfaces deviate far from circular. Since tokamak plasmas are thought to require a divertor defined by a magnetic separatrix, the simplicity and precision that come from the use of ψt as a radial coordinate extend to equilibrium conditions and not just the requirement that ψt be used to determine the implications of Faraday's Law.

      The separatrix of a tokamak with a divertor has several distinct effects: (1) The shaping of the surfaces has a direct effect on the edge safety factor and the internal inductance, which are captured by σ(ψt,t)⁠, a function determined by the shape of the magnetic surfaces, Sec. III. (2) The region near the separatrix is generally characterized by a pedestal5 that enhances the confinement time by an approximate factor of two through sustaining a jump in the plasma temperature and density. This jump produces a strong bootstrap current. The result is a much larger current density at the plasma edge than without a separatrix, which reduces ℓi for a fixed edge safety factor. This makes the plasma more stable to tearing and less stable to external kinks.

      In TFTR, which had near circular magnetic surfaces bounded by a limiter, the plasma current profile was adequately described by the edge safety factor qa and the internal inductance ℓi to determine when disruptions produced by the current profile would occur.3 In JET plasmas,6 which were bounded by a separatrix, the edge safety factor and the internal inductance were not adequate. Nevertheless, the safety factor at the plasma edge gives important information about the plasma disruptivity.

      There is a subtlety. The safety factor q is infinite on a separatrix, which makes the direct use of the value of q on the separatrix of no value in determining disruptivity. In 1989, Taylor et al.7 showed that related information about the stability of plasmas bounded by a separatrix can be obtained by replacing the edge safety factor of limited plasmas by q95⁠. The definition of q95 uses ψpin
      q95≡q((ψpin)95),
      (7)
      where
      (ψpin)95≡ψpinΨpin=0.95,
      (8)
      where Ψpin is the total poloidal magnetic flux produced by the plasma current that lies inside the separatrix. Since tokamak power plants are thought to require a divertor defined by a separatrix, q95 quickly became such a standard designation of the edge rotational transform that is hard to trace it to the reference by Taylor et al.
      The use of 95% of the internal poloidal flux between the magnetic axis and the edge to define the edge safety factor gives a definition that is dependent on the current profile throughout the plasma since1
      ψpin(ψt)=Ψpax+∫0ψtdψtq(ψt)
      (9)
      =Ψpax+∫0ψtI(ψt)σ(ψt)G(ψt)dψt,
      (10)
      using Eq. (29) for the relationship between the safety factor q(ψt) and the shape function σ(ψt)⁠. The toroidal I(ψt) and poloidal G(ψt) currents are defined in Fig. 1.

      The use of a fraction of the toroidal magnetic flux enclosed by the separatrix, Ψt⁠, would have made the q95-like quantity essentially independent of the current profile. The sensitivity of q95 defined by poloidal flux to the breadth of the current profile is illustrated in Fig. 6, which gives ψt/Ψt at the location of 95% of the internal poloidal flux between the magnetic axis and the separatrix, denoted by ψtp95/Ψt⁠, as a function of the width of the current channel. Figure 5 allows one to determine the magnitude of the shape function σ as a function of ψt/Ψt⁠. The use of the poloidal flux gives an uncertainty in the enhancement of σ by the separatrix due to the uncertainty in ψtp95/Ψt⁠. The uncertainty is that 0.89≳ψtp95/Ψt≳0.81⁠. The value of σ is uncertain due to the variation in (ψtp95/Ψt){1+Πσ(ψtp95/Ψt)}⁠, which is between 1.05 and 1.33. This non-trivial definitional uncertainty due to the central current profile could be eliminated by defining the edge by some definite fraction of ψt/Ψt⁠, such as ψt/Ψt=0.85⁠.

      The definition of the internal inductance is also affected by the uncertainty in the definition of the plasma edge. Reference 1 shows that the internal inductance is given by
      ℓi≡∫01I(s)q(1)I(1)q(s)ds,
      (11)
      where s=ψt/ψtp95 with ψtp95 at the place where the poloidal flux reaches 95% of difference in the poloidal flux between the separatrix and the axis.

      The paper Constraints on the magnetic field evolution in tokamak power plants1 was focused on the implications of Faraday's Law, as expressed in Eq. (2), on plasma disruptivity and maintenance. The fundamental errors that are made by those who are not familiar with this equation are pointed out and discussed. This paper has a different focus. It is the study of the shape function σ(ψt,t)⁠, and how it can be calculated. A simple analytic example of a separatrix is derived, which illustrates both the properties σ(ψt,t) in the presence of a separatrix and how they can be calculated. An approximate form of these results was used in Ref. 1 to study the effects of a separatrix on the internal inductance and on the safety factor near the plasma edge.

      Section II defines the plasma current profile, which is thought to be the primary cause of disruptions.

      Section III is on the shape function, σ(ψt,t)⁠. As shown in this section, the shape function in tokamaks fully determines the effect of the shape of the magnetic surfaces on the safety factor and the internal inductance. The shape function was introduced in Sec. V C 4 of Ref. 8. Any deviation of the plasma surface shape from circular increases σ and the safety factor. An example of the effect of elliptical and triangular shaping on σ is derived in this section.

      Section IV introduces the two-wire model of a tokamak with a separatrix, Fig. 4. This model allows an analytic determination of the shape function σ(ψt,t) starting with Cartesian coordinates that are periodic in z. The magnetic surfaces are represented using elliptic functions. The required properties of the elliptic functions and integrals are given in the  Appendix. Subsection IV C derives the shape function in terms of the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, K(k)⁠, Eq. (84). This subsection also derives the expression for the safety factor q=1/ι⁠, where ι is the rotational transform, also in terms of K(k)⁠. Subsection IV D expresses the toroidal flux ψt(k) in terms of E(k)−(1−k2)K(k)⁠, where E(k) is the complete elliptic integral of the second kind, which also determines Ψt⁠, the toroidal magnetic flux enclosed by the separatrix. Subsection IV E obtains the expression of σ(ψt/Ψt)⁠, which leads to Fig. 5. Subsection IV F derives the relation between the location of 95% of ψpin between the magnetic axis and the separatrix and ψt/Ψt as a function of the width of the current channel. This is illustrated in Fig. 6.

      Section V gives a summary, which is short because the fundamental results are also given in the Introduction.

      II. THE PLASMA CURRENT PROFILE

      The profile of the net plasma current is I(ψt,t)/Ip(t)⁠, where the total plasma current is Ip(t)≡I(Ψt,t)⁠, Fig. 1, with Ψt the toroidal flux enclosed by the bounding magnetic surface. It is the current profile that is generally thought to be the primary determinant of whether a plasma is unstable to either tearing or external kink modes. These are thought to be the major causes of disruptions from internal plasma effects.

      Not all disruptions are caused by lack of control of the current profile—a piece of wall material falling into the plasma will lead to a fast radiative collapse of plasma confinement. Nevertheless, it is important to identify parameters that can be measured and controlled to ensure the current profile is stable. In a highly shaped tokamak plasmas, the use of the toroidal rather than the poloidal flux as the radial coordinate simplifies this identification.

      The plasma current density j→ along the magnetic field, or more precisely j||/B≡j→·B→/B2⁠, has two parts. One part, the Pfirsch–Schlüter current, obeys9 the following equation:
      ∂∂ℓj||B=4πdpdψt∂(1/B)∂θ0,
      (12)
      where
      B→=∇→ψt×∇→θ02π.
      (13)

      The Pfirsch–Schlüter current is required to make j→ divergence free in the presence of a gradient in the pressure. The other part is the homogeneous solution to Eq. (12), which is ∂I(ψ,t)/∂ψt and is called the net plasma current. I(ψt,t)≡∫j→·da→φ⁠, Fig. 1, is the toroidal current in Amperes enclosed by a magnetic surface.

      The net plasma current is determined by the loop voltage, through the ηj→ term in an Ohms Law, and by the contributions of the bootstrap current and current drive. The danger of neoclassical tearing modes10,11 limits the usage of the bootstrap current in power plant designs, and the large power12—comparable to the alpha-heating power for full current drive1—results in current drive having a limited roll in power plants.

      III. THE SHAPE FUNCTION

      In axisymmetry, the shape function σ(ψt,t) fully determines the effect of the shape of the magnetic surfaces on the safety factor and the internal inductance.

      The simplest coordinates to give the form of the shape function σ(ψt,t) are the (ψt,θ,φ) coordinates, Fig. 1, in which
      2πB→=∇→ψt×∇→θ+∇→φ×∇→ψp(ψt,t)
      (14)
      =μ0G(ψt,t)∇→φ+μ0I(ψt,t)∇→θ+β∗∇→ψt.
      (15)
      These two simple forms for B→ exist13 whenever or wherever there are magnetic surfaces and a plasma equilibrium, ∇→p=j→×B→⁠. The first or contravariant representation, Eq. (14), makes the divergence-free nature of B→(x→) manifest. The second or covariant form, Eq. (15), allows a simple determination of ∇→×B→⁠. Positions are defined using these coordinates as x→(ψt,θ,φ,t)=XX̂+YŶ+ZẐ⁠, with the Cartesian coordinates specified as functions of (ψt,θ,φ,t)⁠. The mathematics of general coordinates is derived in a two-page appendix to Ref. 2.

      The coordinates (ψt,θ,φ) that have both a simple contravariant, Eq. (14), and covariant, Eq. (15), representation13 are unfamiliar to many working on tokamaks. They are well known as Boozer coordinates to those working on stellarators since they are fundamental to showing that the strong breaking of axisymmetry need not give unacceptable neoclassical confinement as was thought. Indeed, the degree to which the neoclassical transport can be reduced appears remarkably unlimited.14,15

      Reference 1 proves that in axisymmetric tori, the shape function is
      σ(ψt,t)≡(∂x→/∂θ)2(∂x→/∂φ)2.
      (16)
      An analytic study of the behavior of the shape function σ(ψt) in the presence of a separatrix is the focus of this paper.

      A. Generalized coordinates

      Although the shape function has it simplest form in Boozer coordinates, other poloidal and toroidal angles can be defined and will be used in the analytic separatrix example given in Sec. IV.

      The contravariant form of the magnetic field, Eq. (14), is preserved when the toroidal angle preserves its 2π periodicity but otherwise undergoes a general alteration, φ=φ∗+ν(ψt,θ∗,φ∗)⁠, and the poloidal angle θ is changed to θ=θ∗+ι(ψt)ν with ι≡dψp/dψt⁠. Then,
      2πB→=∇→ψt×∇→θ∗+∇→φ∗×∇→ψp(ψt)
      (17)
      =μ0G(ψt)∇→φ∗+μ0I(ψt)∇→θ∗+∇→ϕ+βn∇→ψt,
      (18)
      with
      ϕ≡μ0(G+ιI)ν.
      (19)
      Using the dual relations of general coordinates,8 Eq. (17) can be written using the (ψt,θ∗,φ∗) Jacobian, J∗⁠, as
      2πB→=1J∗(∂x→∂φ∗+ι∂x→∂θ∗),
      (20)
      since dψp/dψt≡ι⁠. The Jacobian can be found by dotting Eq. (17) with ∇→φ∗ to obtain 2πB→·∇→φ∗=(∇→ψt×∇→θ∗)·∇→φ∗⁠. Therefore,
      J∗≡1(∇→ψt×∇→θ∗)·∇→φ∗
      (21)
      =12πB→·∇→φ∗.
      (22)

      B. Relation between the shape function and the safety factor

      The relation between the shape function and the safety factor can be simply derived in (ψt,θ∗,φ∗) coordinates.

      In axisymmetry, (∂x→/∂θ∗)·(∂x→/∂φ∗)=0⁠. Dotting Eqs. (20) and (18) with ∂x→/∂θ∗ and with ∂x→/∂φ∗⁠, respectively, gives
      ι1J∗(∂x→∂θ*)2=μ0I(ψt)+∂ϕ∂θ∗,
      (23)
      1J∗(∂x→∂φ*)2=μ0G(ψt)+∂ϕ∂φ∗.
      (24)
      Integrating both equations over θ∗ and φ∗ through their full 2π ranges implies
      ι∮(∂x→∂θ*)2dθ∗dφ∗J∗=μ0I(ψt)∮dθ∗dφ∗J∗,
      (25)
      ∮(∂x→∂φ*)2dθ∗dφ∗J∗=μ0G(ψt)∮dθ∗dφ∗J∗.
      (26)
      Defining the shape function as
      σ≡∮(∂x→∂θ*)2dθ∗dφ∗J∗∮(∂x→∂φ*)2dθ∗dφ∗J∗
      (27)
      implies
      ισ(ψt)=IG.
      (28)
      Since the safety factor q≡1/ι⁠, the shape function on a magnetic surface is simply related to the safety factor1
      q(ψt,t)=σ(ψt,t)G(ψt,t)I(ψt,t),
      (29)
      where I(ψt,t) is the toroidal current enclosed by the magnetic surface and G(ψt,t) is the poloidal current outside the magnetic surface, the current that comes up through the central hole in a toroidal magnetic surface, Fig. 1.

      (36)

      IV. THE TWO-WIRE MODEL

      The qualitative features of the shape function in the presence of a separatrix will be illustrated by a simple analytic model for an axisymmetric tokamak with a single-null divertor, Fig. 4. The model uses a Cartesian coordinate system in which the z coordinate is assumed to be periodic, z=Rφ∗⁠. R represents the major radius of the torus, and φ∗ is the toroidal angle in a (ψt,θ∗,φ∗) coordinate system as in Subsections III A and III C.

Cylindrical coordinates (rx,θx,z) that have their axis at the X-point of the separatrix are used to define positions, while r gives the distance from the magnetic axis of the plasma—all of the plasma current is assumed to flow in a wire along the magnetic axis.       The poloidal magnetic field is assumed to be produced by two parallel wires at x=0 and y=±a carrying equal currents, which give a divertor-like separatrix with an X-point at x=0⁠, y=0⁠. The top wire is taken to represent the plasma current Ip⁠, and the bottom wire to the external current that is required to have a divertor, Fig. 4. The toroidal field is represented by Bφ≡Bz and assumed to be a spatial constant.

      As the distance r from the wire representing the total plasma current Ip goes to zero, the rotational transform is as follows:
      ι(r)→μ0RIp2πBφr2.
      (37)
      The most distant point on the separatrix is at a distance a from the wire that represents the plasma. Along the separatrix, ι=0⁠, and the safety factor q≡1/ι is infinite.

      In 1968, Hobbs and Taylor16 discussed the properties of magnetic fields produced by straight wires. However, the analysis of Boozer and Rechester17 from 10 years later provides the mathematical basis for obtaining an analytic solution for the shape function σ(ψt,t) using the elliptic functions. The properties of the elliptic functions are not well known in the fusion community. The properties that will be used are given in the  Appendix.

      As illustrated in Fig. 4, the distance from the plasma wire is given by r2=x2+(y−a)2⁠, and the distance from the external wire is given by re2=x2+(y+a)2⁠. The distance from the X-point is given by rx2=x2+y2⁠. Letting x=rx cos θx and y=rx sin θx⁠,
      r2=rx2−2arx sin θx+a2,
      (38)
      re2=rx2+2arx sin θx+a2.
      (39)

      A. Poloidal flux

      The magnetic field produced by the wire carrying the plasma current Ip is
      μ0Ip2πrθ̂=12π∇→φ∗×∇→ψpw
      (40)
      =ẑR×dψpwdrr̂,
      (41)
      so
      dψpwdr=μ0RIpr,
      (42)
      ψpw(r)=μ0RIp∫ardrr
      (43)
      =μ0RIp ln (ra).
      (44)
      Since both wires contribute to the same flux, the total poloidal flux is
      ψpin=μ0RIp2ln (r2re2a4)
      (45)
      =μ0RIp2ln(k2),
      (46)
      where
      k2≡r2re2a4
      (47)
      =((rxa)2−2(rxa)sin θx+1)2((rea)2+2(rea)sin θx+1)2
      (48)
      =(rxa)4+(rxa)2(2−4 sin2(θx))+1
      (49)
      =(rxa)4+2(rxa)2 cos(2θx)+1,
      (50)
      since 2 sin2θx=1−cos(2θx)⁠.

      Since B→·∇→ψpin=0⁠, the magnetic surfaces are the constant-k surfaces. Equation (50) gives the equation for the magnetic surfaces, and Eq. (46) gives the poloidal flux associated with those surfaces.

      Representing the plasma current as lying in a wire makes the poloidal flux ψpin=μ0RIp ln(k) singular. As k→0⁠, the poloidal flux goes to minus infinity and k=2r/a⁠, which follows from re2=(2a)2 as r→0⁠. As the magnetic axis at r=0 is approached, ψpin=−μ0RIp ln(a/2r)⁠, not zero.

      A nonsingular result for the poloidal flux at the magnetic axis can be obtained if the plasma current density is assumed to be constant out to a radius rI≪a⁠, but zero for larger r. The poloidal flux for r≪a with ψpin(r=0)=0⁠, as it should be, is
      ψpin(r)=2πμ0R∫0rBθ(r)dr
      (51)
      =μ0RIp(∫0rIrrI2dr+∫rIrdrr)
      (52)
      =μ0RIp(12+ln (rrI)) when r>rI
      (53)
      =μ0RIp(12+ln (ka2rI))
      (54)
      =μ0RIp ln (ekkI),
      (55)
      where ψpin(k=kI)=12μ0RIp is the smallest value of ψpin for which Eq. (55) is valid since k=kI is at the radius at which the current density drops to zero.

      Although Eq. (55) was derived assuming k≪1⁠, a comparison with Eq. (46) for the poloidal flux shows that it applies for all k>kI⁠.

      In Eq. (46), an additive constant to the poloidal flux was chosen so that the poloidal flux is zero on the separatrix, k=1⁠. Equation (55) implies the poloidal flux between the magnetic axis and the separatrix,
      ψps−Ψpax=μ0RIp ln(ekI)
      (56)
      or
      ψpin(k)=μ0RIp ln(ekkI).
      (57)
      The definition of q95⁠, Eqs. (7) and Eq. (57), implies that the value of k at 95% of the internal poloidal flux produced by the plasma current is
      k95p=(kIe)0.05
      (58)
      or
      kI=ek95p20.
      (59)

      B. Magnetic surface description using Jacobi elliptic functions

      Equation (50) for the shape of the constant-k surfaces, which are the magnetic surfaces, can be represented in terms of the Jacobi elliptic functions by letting
      cos(2θx)=−dn(u)
      (60)
      and
      rx2a2=dn(u)+kcn(u).
      (61)
      Substituting Eqs. (60) and (61) into Eq. (50) demonstrates the identity
      k2=(dn(u)+kcn(u))2−2(dn(u)+kcn(u))dn(u)+1
      (62)
      =−dn2(u)+k2cn2(u)+1
      (63)
      =k2−(k2sn2(u)+dn2(u))+1,
      (64)
      where
      1=k2sn2(u)+dn2(u)
      (65)
      is an identity among the elliptic functions, Eq. (A2), as in 1=cn2(u)+sn2(u)⁠, Eq. (A1).
      Each magnetic surface in the plasma region is defined by k with 1≥k≥0⁠. Poloidal locations on a magnetic surface given by u, with u and u+4K(k) are equivalent positions. That is, u has a 4K(k) periodicity, which can be turned into the 2π periodicity of the poloidal angle θ∗ by the identification,
      θ∗=2π4K(k)u.
      (66)

      Since ψt is a function of k alone, multiplying θ∗ by a k dependent factor does not change the fundamental form of the contravariant, Eq. (17) representations of B→⁠. The freedom of ν implies that the fundamental form of the covariant, Eq. (18), representations of B→ is also unchanged.

      The position vector is

      D. Toroidal flux ψt(k) and σ(ψt/Ψt) calculations

      The toroidal magnetic flux enclosed by a magnetic surface is
      ψt(k)=∫0kdψpindkι(k)dk
      (90)

      (92)

      V. SUMMARY

      The toroidal magnetic flux ψt enclosed by each magnetic surface is rarely used as a radial coordinate in the tokamak literature. The preferred choice is the difference in the poloidal magnetic flux associated with a magnetic surface and that at the magnetic axis. Faraday's Law says the slippage of the poloidal relative to toroidal flux is the loop voltage, Eq. (2). It seems impossible to avoid confusion about the evolution of magnetic fields in a theory that ignores the toroidal flux.

      The focus on the poloidal flux in axisymmetric systems, such as tokamaks, comes from the Grad–Shafranov equation, which determines the poloidal flux as a function of position x→ without a direct reference to the toroidal flux. Although the toroidal flux and Ψpax(t) could be calculated, generally they are not.

      A divertor defined by a separatrix is thought to be essential in tokamak power plants. Research is required to determine what parameters can be used in tokamak power plants to ensure an acceptably low rate of disruptions. As has been shown in this paper, both the edge safety factor and the internal inductance have a large but unnecessary definitional uncertainty when defined using the poloidal flux rather than the toroidal flux. Defining the edge using a criterion based on 95% of the poloidal flux corresponds to using approximately 85% of the toroidal flux. It is unclear what fraction of the toroidal flux is optimal for determining the disruptivity of tokamak plasmas.

      Artificial Intelligence (AI) could be used with large existing datasets from tokamak experiments as a basis for identifying parameters that ensure an acceptably low disruption probability. Computational studies are of far lower cost than experiments for generating data and could be used to determine the linear tearing and external kink mode stability18,19 that could credibly arise during the plasma evolution of proposed tokamak power plants. Remarkably, neither approach for identifying critical parameters is a focus of research. For example, the recent paper on ARC disruption physics and strategy20 focused on disruption mitigation and survival. A strategy for disruption avoidance was not discussed.
